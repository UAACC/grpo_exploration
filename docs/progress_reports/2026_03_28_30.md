# Progress Report: March 2026

## Summary

This report summarizes a month of systematic investigation into improving offline GRPO for LLM post-training on mathematical reasoning. Our goal is to make offline GRPO work: train a weak student (0.5B) using pre-collected rollouts from a strong teacher (7B), achieving performance gains comparable to online GRPO but at half the computational cost.

Through controlled experiments, we progressively ruled out implementation bugs, distribution mismatch, and exposure bias as explanations for offline GRPO's failure. We then developed DG-offline (an adaptation of the Delightful Policy Gradient), which became the first offline method to expand the set of problems for which the student's sampling distribution contains a correct solution (pass@16: 61.4% → 64.2%). In parallel, generation-level analysis revealed that for 61% of MATH problems, the correct reasoning path already exists in the untrained student's sampling distribution (pass@16 with oracle selection). The student's greedy decoding selects a suboptimal problem-solving strategy at early branching points, where the teacher's preferred token is typically the student's second-ranked choice (mean rank 2.0, mean probability 22.5%). This suggests that for the majority of problems, the core challenge is not transferring new knowledge from the teacher but shifting the student's greedy preference toward strategies it already assigns substantial probability to. For the remaining 39% of problems where no correct path exists in the student's distribution, genuine knowledge transfer remains necessary.

## 1. DG-Offline: Delightful Policy Gradient for Offline GRPO

### 1.1 Motivation

Standard offline GRPO weights gradients by the IS ratio π_student/π_teacher with PPO clipping. This treats surprising successes and failures symmetrically. Based on Osband (2026, arXiv:2603.20521), we implemented DG-offline, which replaces IS ratios with a sigmoid gate on delight = advantage × surprisal, computed entirely from the learner's current policy.

### 1.2 Implementation

Code: `DG-offline/trainer.py`, `DG-offline/train.py`. Technical doc: `docs/dg_offline/technical_reference.md` (knowledge hub at `docs/dg_offline/`).

Key mechanism: gate = σ(advantage × mean_surprisal / η), applied to advantages before TRL's loss computation. IS ratio neutralized by setting old_logps = current_logps.

### 1.5 Key Observations

1. **The ranking of methods is different between MATH and GSM8K.**
   - On MATH, DG-offline η=0.5 is the best offline method on both greedy (29.00%) and pass@16 (64.20%), with online GRPO slightly ahead.
   - On GSM8K, Mixture B is the best offline method on greedy (51.11%) and pass@1 (49.73%), while DG-offline η=1.0 is the best offline method on pass@16 (85.67%). Online GRPO (cross-task MATH checkpoint) still dominates greedy at 55.82%.
   - The claim "DG-offline is the best offline method" is MATH-specific and does not hold on GSM8K.

2. **BC behaves differently on the two datasets.**
   - On MATH, BC is within noise of baseline on all three metrics (27.40% vs 27.16% greedy).
   - On GSM8K, BC genuinely helps: +1.51pp greedy (49.67% vs 48.16%) for BC-all and +1.27pp for BC-correct-only. BC also boosts temp=0.6 pass@1 by 2.96-4.70pp.
   - Likely explanation: on GSM8K the teacher is 95.1% correct (vs 70.9% on MATH), so the teacher data is much cleaner and BC's uniform cross-entropy gets more signal per gradient step.

3. **No offline method expands the pass@16 ceiling on GSM8K.**
   - GSM8K baseline pass@16 is 86.13%. Every trained model sits below this, in the 83.17%-85.67% range.
   - On MATH, DG-offline η=0.5 and online GRPO both pushed pass@16 above baseline (64.2% and 64.4% vs 61.4%). That expansion does not replicate on GSM8K.
   - Likely because GSM8K baseline pass@16 is already very high (86%), leaving little headroom for any method to add new solvable problems.

4. **η=0.5 is MATH-specific; GSM8K has a different sweet spot.**
   - MATH: DG η=0.5 peaks pass@16 at 64.20%, and the trend is monotone from both sides.
   - GSM8K: DG η=1.0 peaks pass@16 at 85.67%. η=0.5 is actually the WORST DG variant on GSM8K pass@16 (83.17%).
   - The optimal η appears to depend on the dataset's surprisal distribution, not a universal constant.

5. **Why smaller η worked on MATH (training dynamics analysis):** η=0.5 results in KL=0.011 at end of training (small, targeted drift), while η=2.0 results in KL=0.040 (5x more drift, in worse directions). Smaller η suppresses gradients from harmful samples (surprising failures), preventing the model from drifting in bad directions. The model moves less overall but in better directions. We have not repeated this analysis for GSM8K, so we cannot explain why η=1.0 is the GSM8K sweet spot.

| | Teacher Correct | Teacher Wrong |
|---|---|---|
| **Student Correct** | 64 (32%) | 0 (0%) |

### 2.3 Divergence Analysis

For all 84 gap problems, the student and teacher diverge at the very first line (100% early divergence). They take completely different problem-solving strategies from the start.

Examples from the data:
- **Hexagon problem**: Student computes "3 × 21 = 63" (wrong: treats perimeter as side length). Teacher computes "21/3 = 7" (correct factoring).
- **Polynomial roots**: Student uses Vieta's formulas and gets stuck in 2048 tokens. Teacher immediately factors x³+ax²+ax+1 = (x+1)(x²+(a-1)x+1) and solves in 695 tokens.
- **Estimation problem**: Student estimates numerically and gets 4. Teacher uses the conjugate trick and gets 13535.

Despite these completely different strategies, the overall token-level agreement remains 92%. A plausible explanation is that once a strategy is chosen, the rest of the completion follows a locally coherent chain of reasoning, and the teacher's next-token predictions align with the student's continuation regardless of whether the strategy is correct. However, we have not directly verified this interpretation by measuring agreement separately on pre-divergence vs post-divergence tokens.

### 2.4 Branching Point Analysis

**Experiment**: `bc/analyze_branching.py`, job 4544913 (82 gap problems)

At the first divergence token for each gap problem, we examined both models' full probability distributions.

| Metric | Value |
|--------|-------|
| Teacher's token rank in student's distribution (mean) | **2.0** |
| Teacher's token in student's top-5 | **92.7%** (76/82) |
| Teacher's token in student's top-10 | **96.3%** (79/82) |
| Teacher's token probability under student (mean) | **22.5%** |
| Student's token rank in teacher's distribution (mean) | 5.3 |

At the first divergence token, the teacher's preferred token is almost always present in the student's top-5 (92.7%), just not as the greedy argmax. The divergence tokens are typically near-synonyms ("need" vs "start", "identify" vs "determine") that appear stylistically interchangeable but lead to completely different reasoning chains.

Note: the 22.5% probability is measured only at the first divergence token. Successfully completing a correct solution requires correct choices at potentially multiple subsequent branching points, so the per-sample probability of a fully correct completion is lower than 22.5%. The empirically observed pass@16 of 61.4% reflects this compounding.

DG-offline (η=0.5) and online GRPO are the only methods that raise pass@16 on MATH (from 61.4% to ~64%), suggesting they expand the set of problems for which the student's distribution contains a correct solution path.

## 4. Why BC Fails

BC trains on ~24M tokens (48K completions × ~500 tokens each) with uniform cross-entropy loss. The divergence analysis suggests that the student and teacher choose different strategies early in the completion (first divergence token ranges from position 1 to ~42 in our data, with most occurring in the first 30 tokens). These early tokens constitute a small fraction of the total sequence, so their gradient contribution is diluted by the majority of tokens where the student already agrees with the teacher.

Evidence: After BC training, top-1 agreement improved from 93.06% to 93.32% (+0.26%, measured on teacher context, 200 samples). This small shift is consistent with BC primarily reducing loss on tokens the student already predicts well, rather than meaningfully changing preferences at the critical early positions. However, we have not directly measured whether BC changes the student's top-1 preference specifically at branching points; this would require running the branching analysis on the BC-trained model.

## 5. Evolution of Understanding

This section traces how our understanding evolved over the course of the project, with each phase building on evidence from the previous one.

### Phase 1: Method comparison (early March)
**Question**: Can offline GRPO with teacher rollouts improve the student?
**Finding**: No. All offline methods degrade or stall on MATH. Only online GRPO helps slightly (+1.7%).
**Hypotheses formed**: Distribution mismatch (H1), advantage collapse (H2).

### Phase 2: Isolating the cause (mid March)
**Question**: Is the degradation from distribution mismatch or from the offline algorithm itself?
**Finding**: Teacher-self offline GRPO (7B on its own rollouts, no mismatch) also degrades (-3.8%). Online GRPO for the 7B teacher is flat. Advantage collapse is severe (82-93% of groups have zero reward variance).
**Hypotheses updated**: Distribution mismatch is not the sole cause. Rollout staleness and advantage collapse are implicated.

### Phase 3: Implementation verification (mid March)
**Question**: Is there a bug in our training pipeline?
**Finding**: 9 automated checks passed for BC. Single-sample overfit works. Stored logprobs match teacher recomputation. No bugs found.
**Conclusion**: The degradation is a real algorithmic/data phenomenon, not an implementation error.

### Phase 4: Token-level analysis (late March)
**Question**: Why can't BC add knowledge despite 70% correct teacher data?
**Finding**: 93% token-level agreement between student and teacher. Disagreement tokens are stylistic. BC barely moves the distribution (+0.26% agreement after training).
**Initial interpretation**: Student and teacher are already close at the token level. The gap must be in long-horizon coherence.

### Phase 5: Falsifying the exposure bias hypothesis (late March)
**Question**: Does the student's own context degrade alignment with the teacher?
**Finding**: Agreement on student-generated context is 92.10%, nearly identical to teacher context (93.06%). No decay with position.
**Conclusion**: Exposure bias is not the explanation.

### Phase 6: Generation-level analysis (late March)
**Question**: If tokens are 92% the same, why is accuracy 3x worse?
**Finding**: On 84 gap problems, student and teacher diverge at the first line in 100% of cases. At the first divergence token, the teacher's preferred token is in the student's top-5 with 92.7% frequency (mean rank 2.0, mean probability 22.5%). pass@16 (oracle) is 61.4% vs 26.8% greedy.
**Conclusion**: For the majority of problems, the correct reasoning path exists in the student's sampling distribution. Greedy decoding selects a suboptimal strategy at early branching points.

### Phase 7: DG-offline (late March)
**Question**: Can we design an offline method that actually adds knowledge?
**Finding**: DG-offline with η=0.5 is the only offline method that raises both pass@1 (+2.2%) and pass@16 (+2.8%) on MATH. It works by asymmetrically filtering gradients, suppressing harmful updates from surprising failures while amplifying useful updates from surprising successes.

### Current understanding

The correct reasoning path exists in the student's sampling distribution for 61% of MATH problems (pass@16, oracle). The student cannot reliably select it: greedy decoding yields only 27%. At strategy-selection branching points, the teacher's approach is rank 2 in the student's distribution with 22.5% probability, meaning the student assigns substantial probability to the correct strategy but does not prefer it over alternatives. Standard offline training methods fail because they optimize all tokens uniformly, but the critical tokens that determine strategy constitute less than 1% of the sequence and are drowned out by continuation tokens. DG-offline helps because its asymmetric filtering concentrates signal on informative samples. Online GRPO helps because it learns from the student's own successful explorations, directly reinforcing correct paths.

## 6. Open Questions for Improving Offline GRPO

The findings above point to specific directions for making offline GRPO more effective:

### 6.1 Targeted training on strategy-selection tokens

BC and offline GRPO train on all tokens uniformly, but only the first ~10 tokens (strategy selection) determine correctness. Can we concentrate the training signal where it matters?

Possible approaches:
- Position-weighted cross-entropy for BC: 10x weight on the first 20 completion tokens
- Train only on the first N tokens of each teacher completion, ignoring the continuation
- Use the branching point analysis to identify and upweight critical tokens specifically

### 6.2 Leveraging the student's existing knowledge through search

For 61% of MATH problems, the correct solution exists somewhere in the student's top-16 samples, but the student has no way to identify which sample is correct without an oracle. Can we build a practical selection mechanism?

Possible approaches:
- Majority voting: sample N completions, take the most common boxed answer
- Train a lightweight verifier on (problem, completion, correct/incorrect) triples from the student's own samples
- Use the teacher as a verifier (score student completions by teacher log-probability)

### 6.3 Designing a new gradient weighting module for cross-model offline RL

DG-offline showed that replacing IS ratios with a learner-side signal works. But DG was designed for stale actors (small distribution shifts), not cross-model distillation (large distribution gaps). Our setting has specific properties that a purpose-built module should exploit:

- The student's correct strategy is at rank 2 with 22.5% probability at branching points
- Token-level surprisal is low (~0.2 nats) because most tokens are continuation, not strategy-selection
- The critical signal is concentrated in the first ~10 tokens of each completion
- 80-90% of prompt groups have zero reward variance (advantage collapse)

A custom weighting module should be designed for these specific characteristics rather than borrowing from a different setting. Possible design directions:
- Weight by position: higher weight on early tokens where strategy is selected
- Weight by token-level information gain: upweight tokens where the student's distribution is most uncertain (high entropy) rather than using surprisal
- Weight by advantage magnitude × strategy divergence: gate based on whether this sample would change the student's greedy strategy at the branching point
- Combine DG's asymmetric filtering with strategy-aware token weighting

### 6.4 Scaling to stronger students

All results are on the 0.5B student. The branching analysis shows the teacher's strategy is at rank 2 with 22.5% probability. For a stronger student (1.5B, 3B), this probability may be higher, meaning offline methods might work better when the student-teacher gap is smaller.

Possible experiments:
- 1.5B student with 3B teacher rollouts (same vocab, no OOV issue)
- Repeat the branching analysis: does rank drop from 2.0 to 1.x?
- If so, even standard offline GRPO might work for stronger students

### 6.5 Understanding why online GRPO works

Online GRPO is the only method that raises both pass@1 and pass@16. What specifically does it learn that offline methods cannot?

Possible analyses:
- Run the branching point analysis on the online-GRPO-trained model: did the teacher's strategy move from rank 2 to rank 1?
- Compare the student's generation distribution before and after online GRPO: which strategies were reinforced?
- Measure whether online GRPO reduces the number of gap problems or changes their character
