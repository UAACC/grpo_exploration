# Progress Report: March 2026

## Summary

This report summarizes a month of systematic investigation into offline reinforcement learning for LLM post-training. Through a series of controlled experiments — spanning multiple training methods, extensive diagnostic analyses, and a novel adaptation of the Delightful Policy Gradient — we arrived at a finding that reframes the research direction: the 0.5B student already possesses enough knowledge to solve 61% of MATH problems. The bottleneck is not knowledge transfer from the teacher, but strategy selection during greedy decoding. This insight emerged from progressively ruling out alternative explanations (implementation bugs, distribution mismatch, advantage collapse, exposure bias) and converging on direct empirical evidence through generation-level analysis.

---

## 1. DG-Offline: Delightful Policy Gradient for Offline GRPO

### 1.1 Motivation

Standard offline GRPO weights gradients by the IS ratio π_student/π_teacher with PPO clipping. This treats surprising successes and failures symmetrically. Based on Osband (2026, arXiv:2603.20521), we implemented DG-offline which replaces IS ratios with a sigmoid gate on delight = advantage × surprisal, computed entirely from the learner's current policy.

### 1.2 Implementation

- Code: `DG-offline/trainer.py`, `DG-offline/train.py`
- Technical doc: `dg-offline_imp.md`
- Key mechanism: gate = σ(advantage × mean_surprisal / η), applied to advantages before TRL's loss computation. IS ratio neutralized by setting old_logps = current_logps.

### 1.3 Results — MATH (500 problems, Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct)

### 1.5 Key Observations

1. **DG-offline (η=0.5) is the best offline method on MATH.** It is the only offline method that improves both pass@1 (+2.2%) and pass@16 (+2.8%) over baseline. Standard offline methods (BC, offline GRPO, mixtures) leave pass@16 unchanged — they shuffle greedy preferences but don't add knowledge.

2. **η=0.5 is the sweet spot on MATH.** The trend is non-monotonic: η=0.1 is too aggressive (pass@16=62.4%), η=0.5 peaks (64.2%), η=2.0 is too soft (62.2%).

3. **GSM8K shows weaker DG effects.** All DG etas are within noise of baseline on GSM8K. The baseline is already at 48% (vs 27% on MATH), so there's less room for offline methods to help. Online GRPO still dominates on GSM8K (+7.6%).

4. **Why smaller η works better (analysis of training dynamics):**
   - η=0.5: KL=0.011 at end of training (small, targeted drift)
   - η=2.0: KL=0.040 at end of training (5x more drift, in worse directions)
   - Smaller η suppresses gradient from harmful samples (surprising failures), preventing the model from drifting in bad directions. The model moves less overall but in better directions.

## 2. Generation Gap Analysis

### 2.1 Token Agreement on Student Context

**Experiment**: `bc/agreement_on_student_ctx.py`, job 4543743

We measured whether the 93% token-level agreement (previously measured on teacher-generated context) holds when the student generates its own context.

| Context source | Token agreement | Source |
|---------------|----------------|--------|
| Teacher-generated prefix | 93.06% | `bc/check_p4.py`, job 4383020 |
| Student-generated prefix | **92.10%** | `bc/agreement_on_student_ctx.py`, job 4543743 |
| Gap | **0.96 pp** | |

**Finding**: Agreement is nearly identical regardless of who generated the context. The exposure bias hypothesis (that the student's own context degrades alignment) is **falsified**. Agreement also does not decay with position — it slightly increases from 91.4% (tokens 0-49) to 94.6% (tokens 550-599).

### 2.2 Quadrant Analysis

**Experiment**: `bc/analyze_gap.py`, job 4543742 (200 MATH test problems)

| | Teacher Correct | Teacher Wrong |
|---|---|---|
| **Student Correct** | 64 (32%) | 0 (0%) |
| **Student Wrong** | **84 (42%)** | 52 (26%) |

- Student accuracy: 32%, Teacher accuracy: 74%
- The "gap" = 84 problems where the teacher is correct and the student is wrong
- Student NEVER outperforms the teacher (0 problems in "student only" quadrant)

### 2.3 Divergence Analysis

For all 84 gap problems, the student and teacher **diverge at the very first line** (100% early divergence). They take completely different problem-solving strategies from the start.

Examples from the data:
- **Hexagon problem**: Student computes "3 × 21 = 63" (wrong: treats perimeter as side length). Teacher computes "21/3 = 7" (correct factoring).
- **Polynomial roots**: Student uses Vieta's formulas and gets stuck in 2048 tokens. Teacher immediately factors x³+ax²+ax+1 = (x+1)(x²+(a-1)x+1) and solves in 695 tokens.
- **Estimation problem**: Student estimates numerically and gets 4. Teacher uses the conjugate trick and gets 13535.

Despite these completely different strategies, token-level agreement is 92% because once a strategy is chosen, the rest of the completion is internally coherent and both models agree on filler tokens.

### 2.4 Branching Point Analysis

**Experiment**: `bc/analyze_branching.py`, job 4544913 (82 gap problems)

At the first divergence token for each gap problem, we examined both models' full probability distributions.

| Metric | Value |
|--------|-------|
| Teacher's token rank in student's distribution (mean) | **2.0** |
| Teacher's token in student's top-5 | **92.7%** (76/82) |
| Teacher's token in student's top-10 | **96.3%** (79/82) |
| Teacher's token probability under student (mean) | **22.5%** |
| Student's token rank in teacher's distribution (mean) | 5.3 |

**The teacher's correct strategy is almost always available in the student's distribution — it's just not the greedy argmax.** The divergence tokens are typically near-synonyms: "need" vs "start", "identify" vs "determine", "express" vs "determine". But these seemingly innocent word choices lead to completely different reasoning chains.

**Best-of-N implication**: With 22.5% probability per sample of taking the correct path, sampling N=16 times gives P(at least one correct) ≈ 98%.

## 3. Best-of-N Evaluation

### 3.1 Results

The best-of-N evaluation (sample N completions at temp=0.6, check if any is correct) reveals the student's knowledge ceiling:

**MATH:**
| Model | pass@1 | pass@16 | Gap to teacher |
|-------|--------|---------|---------------|
| Student greedy | 26.8% | — | 48.2 pp |
| Student best-of-16 | — | 61.4% | **13.6 pp** |
| Teacher greedy | 75.0% | — | — |

**GSM8K:**
| Model | pass@1 | pass@16 | Gap to teacher |
|-------|--------|---------|---------------|
| Student greedy | 48.2% | — | 47.5 pp |
| Student best-of-16 | — | 86.1% | **9.6 pp** |
| Teacher greedy | 95.7% | — | — |

### 3.2 Implications

1. **The untrained student already has the knowledge to solve 61% of MATH and 86% of GSM8K.** The gap to the teacher shrinks from ~48pp to ~10-14pp with just 16 samples.

2. **All standard offline training methods (BC, offline GRPO, mixtures) leave the knowledge ceiling unchanged.** pass@16 stays at ~61% regardless of training. These methods only change which path the greedy decoder picks — they don't add or remove problem-solving strategies.

3. **DG-offline (η=0.5) and online GRPO are the only methods that raise the knowledge ceiling** on MATH (from 61.4% to ~64%). They genuinely teach the student new approaches.

4. **pass@16 is an oracle upper bound** (requires knowing the correct answer to select the right sample). Practical alternatives that don't need an oracle:
   - Majority voting (sample N, take most common answer)
   - Reward model scoring (sample N, pick highest-scored completion)
   - Online GRPO (train the model to put more probability on the correct path so greedy picks it)

## 4. Why BC Fails — Token-Level Cross-Entropy vs Strategy Selection

BC trains on ~24M tokens (48K completions × 500 tokens each) with uniform cross-entropy loss. But the accuracy gap lives in ~5-10 strategy-selection tokens at the start of each completion (<1% of total tokens). The gradient signal for these critical tokens is diluted by the 99% of filler tokens the student already predicts correctly.

Evidence: After BC training, top-1 agreement improved from 93.06% to 93.32% (+0.26%). BC barely moved the distribution — it improved filler prediction, not strategy selection.

## 5. Evolution of Understanding

This section traces how our understanding evolved over the course of the project, with each phase building on evidence from the previous one.

### Phase 1: Method comparison (early March)
**Question**: Can offline GRPO with teacher rollouts improve the student?
**Finding**: No. All offline methods degrade or stall on MATH. Only online GRPO helps slightly (+1.7%).
**Hypotheses formed**: Distribution mismatch (H1), advantage collapse (H2).

### Phase 2: Isolating the cause (mid March)
**Question**: Is the degradation from distribution mismatch or from the offline algorithm itself?
**Finding**: Teacher-self offline GRPO (7B on its own rollouts, no mismatch) also degrades (-3.8%). Online GRPO for the 7B teacher is flat. Advantage collapse is severe (82-93% of groups have zero reward variance).
**Hypotheses updated**: Distribution mismatch is not the sole cause. Rollout staleness and advantage collapse are implicated.

### Phase 3: Implementation verification (mid March)
**Question**: Is there a bug in our training pipeline?
**Finding**: 9 automated checks passed for BC. Single-sample overfit works. Stored logprobs match teacher recomputation. No bugs found.
**Conclusion**: The degradation is a real algorithmic/data phenomenon, not an implementation error.

### Phase 4: Deep diagnostic — token-level analysis (late March)
**Question**: Why can't BC add knowledge despite 70% correct teacher data?
**Finding**: 93% token-level agreement between student and teacher. Disagreement tokens are stylistic. BC barely moves the distribution (+0.26% agreement after training).
**Initial interpretation**: Student and teacher are already close at the token level. The gap must be in long-horizon coherence.

### Phase 5: Falsifying our own hypothesis (late March)
**Question**: Is exposure bias the explanation? (Student trained on teacher context but tested on own context)
**Finding**: Agreement on student-generated context is 92.10% — nearly identical to teacher context (93.06%). No decay with position. **Exposure bias falsified.**

### Phase 6: The breakthrough — generation-level analysis (late March)
**Question**: If tokens are 92% the same, why is accuracy 3x worse?
**Finding**: On 84 "gap" problems, student and teacher diverge at line 0 in 100% of cases. They choose completely different problem-solving strategies. The teacher's strategy is in the student's top-5 at 92.7% of branching points (mean rank 2.0, mean probability 22.5%). Best-of-16 sampling raises accuracy from 27% to 61%.
**Conclusion**: The student already knows the math. The bottleneck is strategy selection during greedy decoding, not knowledge or capacity.

### Phase 7: DG-offline — a principled offline method (late March)
**Question**: Can we design an offline method that actually adds knowledge?
**Finding**: DG-offline with η=0.5 is the only offline method that raises both pass@1 (+2.2%) and pass@16 (+2.8%) on MATH. It works by asymmetrically filtering gradients — suppressing harmful updates from surprising failures while amplifying useful updates from surprising successes.

### Current understanding
- **The student already knows most of the math.** It can solve 61% of MATH if given 16 attempts.
- **The failure is in decoding, not knowledge.** At strategy-selection branching points, the teacher's approach is rank 2 in the student's distribution with 22.5% probability.
- **Standard offline training methods fail because they optimize the wrong thing.** They train on all tokens uniformly, but the critical tokens (<1%) that determine problem-solving strategy are drowned out by filler.
- **DG-offline helps because its asymmetric filtering** concentrates signal on useful samples (surprising successes) while suppressing harmful ones (surprising failures).
- **Online GRPO helps because it learns from the student's own successful explorations**, directly reinforcing correct paths in the student's distribution.
